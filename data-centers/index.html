HPC · CINECA Marconi100 · 2.5-year telemetry archive

Inside a multi-vendor data center, the edges between systems have no owner. That is where assurance has to live.

We ran Nervous Machine on the one thing you can rarely see: a real multi-vendor supercomputer's full cooling telemetry — IT compute, air cooling, liquid cooling, and weather. Five independent owners, 2.5 years, the CINECA Marconi100 archive. The premise it tests is simple: autonomy you can trust has to know what it doesn't know, and direct its learning there. On this archive it sorted the cooling relationships into the ones safe to automate and the ones that silently decay, surfaced one risk no single vendor can observe, and — on its own — found a variable missing from its own model and corrected it.

What we found in the cooling graph

Which cooling relationships you can trust to automate — and which quietly fall apart.

We watched each relationship in the cooling system across ten operating regimes over 2.5 years and asked one question per edge: can you trust it? The answers fell into four clean classes — safe to learn once, decays over time, missing a variable, or unlearnable — and each one calls for a different response. This isn't anecdotal: an edge's variability across regimes predicts how badly it fails on an unseen regime (ρ = 0.76), and the pattern survives the strict version of the test, on the well-specified edges only (ρ = 0.80). The map is the deliverable.

The drift trap

Predictions look healthy. The control law goes silently stale.

The DRIFTING class is the finding most likely to bite an operator, because it never shows up on a dashboard. Predictions on these edges stay accurate across the whole 2.5-year window — their error is on par with the rock-solid INVARIANT edges. By every health metric most teams watch, they look fine.

The coefficients tell a different story. The starkest case is node_power → fan_speed: its slope fell from approximately 1.3 in winter 2021 to approximately 0.16 in winter 2022 — an 8× monotone decline, almost certainly real control-firmware evolution rather than noise. relRMSE is normalized by target magnitude, so it under-weights exactly this failure mode: the prediction can track the target while the relationship underneath it changes.

A twin used for prediction looks fine and is fine. The same twin used as a control law — inverting the relationship to choose an actuator setting — is silently stale, acting on a coefficient that no longer holds.

No feature fixes a temporal drift of this kind — only continual recalibration does. The audit identifies which edges have this property before deployment; the online recalibration loop is what closes them.

The non-owned edge

Each owner has an inner loop. No outer loop exists between owners. The edges between them are structurally invisible.

The Marconi100 cooling system has five telemetry owners: two IT-side (compute, thermal) and three facility-side (Vertiv air cooling, Schneider liquid cooling, external weather). Each owner runs their own inner loop — their own controller, their own model, their own data tap. DCIM platforms aggregate polled summary metrics across vendors for dashboards and PUE / capacity reporting; the raw closed-loop control telemetry needed to learn or even observe the edges between vendors stays inside each vendor's appliance. No outer loop exists at that granularity. The CINECA M100 archive is structurally rare in the field — the Borghesi et al. data paper calls it the largest public dataset of its kind and notes that public data in this field is mostly kept on premises — which makes it one of the few archives where the cross-owner edge is even visible.

A digital twin built inside one silo treats the other silos the way a self-driving car treats a stoplight: as a point observation of the environment, not as an autonomously controlled system with its own logic. Both views can be wrong in the same way, at the same time, on the edge between them — and neither system can see it.

We tested an early Phase-0 hypothesis that the two cooling loops coordinate complementarily under load. The pattern held for one month and did not generalize: the two winter regime reps disagree in sign, spring is complementary throughout, summer is decoupled. There is no stable coordination policy for any single model to learn in this data. Two claims follow, kept separate because they have different evidential standing:

Measured

Across the instrumented regimes, coordination between the two loops is non-stationary. The one-month flip is not a learnable invariant. We tested and rejected the obvious artifact explanation — the pattern survives excluding non-cooling units.

Structural

This coordination lives in the one edge no single owner instruments — neither vendor sees the other loop's ΔT — so even detecting whether the two loops have drifted out of their jointly-validated regime is impossible from inside either silo. It requires a cross-owner layer to observe at all.

The non-stationarity itself isn't novel — it's the well-known central problem of multi-agent learning, where each adapting agent makes the environment non-stationary for the others. The standard mitigations (multi-agent RL, federated digital twins) all assume you either control the agents or they cooperate. Hyperscalers handle this by collapsing the stakeholders into one organization that owns the full stack. Everyone else — supercomputer centers, enterprise data centers, colocation operators — lives with the cross-vendor reality. M100 is the rare archive where the non-owned edge is even observable; in the typical multi-vendor environment it isn't, which means twins built inside today's silos are structurally blind there — whether or not they look healthy on every metric the silo's owner can see.

It improves its own model

The system found a variable missing from its own model — and proved it against the data before adding it.

Knowing what it doesn't know isn't a slogan here; it's a running list. Every relationship carries a live certainty, and the ones that stay uncertain — or stay confident but consistently a little off — collect into what we call the curiosity surface: the boundary of what the system can't yet explain. On this archive, one relationship sat there flagged — node_power → dimm_temp predicted memory temperature confidently, but with a small, stubborn bias. Confident and consistently off by a hair: the signature of a missing variable.

So the system formed a hypothesis — the missing factor is the air coming into the rack — and tested it against the telemetry rather than asserting it. It held: adding inlet temperature lifted that relationship's certainty from 0.76 to 1.0 and cut its error 65%. The new edge was added to the model. No one told it a variable was missing; it noticed, proposed, checked, and patched.

It also proposed two fixes that failed — a cooling-zone boundary to explain a collapsed edge, and hot-aisle recirculation between neighboring racks — and the data rejected both. It doesn't manufacture certainty to look productive; it earns the patch, or leaves the gap flagged and open. That discipline is the point.

This isn't a one-off tuned to cooling. The same compose → learn → escalate loop has run autonomously across five unrelated public domains — battery aging, telescope photometry, power-grid frequency, river flow, urban air quality — converging in four (the fifth correctly refused, and we report it as a negative). On NASA Mars surface-radiation data, the same machinery learned two physical effects that were never written into its prior. Same primitives, different worlds.

Honest scope

What it claims, what it doesn't — and the times the data told us we were wrong.

Effect size is small-n

The headline correlation is computed on 8 edges; the within-subset robustness check on 5 edges (p = 0.10). Both are proof-of-principle, not population estimates. The within-subset survival rules out the shared-cause confound between WEAK edges and held-out error, but a single rank swap moves the result.

The twin consequence is a prediction, not a tested result

The object here is the causal layer, and the edge classes are measured properties of this data. The step from "this relationship is fragile" to "a twin trained on it would fail there" is a reasonable prediction we have not tested against a deployed twin — none was available to us. What is measured is the proxy: an edge's cross-regime variance predicts its own held-out error (ρ = 0.76). Putting the audit head-to-head with a production twin is the obvious next step.

Two of our own mid-stream hypotheses were rejected by the data:

The load-flip didn't generalize

We hypothesized that liquid and air loops coordinate complementarily under load. The pattern held for one month and did not survive cross-regime testing. We report it as a negative.

The climate hasn't shifted

We checked whether the training envelope had drifted. Summer max is flat at 38°C, mean shift is +0.2°C, 0% of post-window summer hours exceed the training max. The archive isn't stale — because it captured the 2022 heatwave by luck of the collection window. We report this as a negative too; an archive ending in 2021 would not have passed.

Other bounds: single weather station, monthly windows, linear edge forms. Nonlinear couplings appear as un-validated edges, not false ones. Facility instrumentation begins 2021-04, so the five-owner graph is only complete from then onward; the feature-availability audit that surfaces this is the most portable result — any team can run it on any archive before training a single line of model code.

DCIM / BMS · facility-management platforms

An anomaly signal that watches the edges, not the sensors

Per-sensor alerting fires on individual telemetry channels. The causal layer fires on coupling drift between channels — the failure mode where the sensors all look fine but the relationship between them has changed. For multi-vendor sites, the outer loop adds anomaly flags on edges that span organizational boundaries, which per-channel monitoring can't see at all.

On-site architecture

In-situ learning per silo. Flags for every edge — including the ones no owner can see.

The substrate's runtime is a deterministic per-edge causal-learning framework, small enough to deploy inside each owner's silo. There is no neural network making predictions, no gradient descent during operation, no model weights to retrain in production. The prior and validation functions can be composed offline by whatever method an operator prefers — engineering rules, published literature, or LLM-assisted authoring against the substrate's API. Once composed, the runtime is deterministic. Each silo runs its own instance on-premises; raw telemetry never leaves the silo it was generated in. The only artifacts that cross organizational boundaries are compact per-edge state deltas (W, Z updates) — small enough to share, abstract enough that they don't leak the underlying telemetry.

Inner loop — per silo

Each owner runs their own instance against their own telemetry. The substrate maintains per-edge W and Z on every causal edge inside the silo, triggers operator-readable flags when certainty stalls or residuals persist, and runs continuously in the background of the existing DCIM / BMS stack. No replacement, no integration battle — an overlay.

Outer loop — across silos

Per-silo instances publish compact state-delta messages to a shared assurance layer. That layer maintains the cross-owner edges — the ones no single silo can see — and surfaces flags when joint regimes drift out of their validated envelope. Without it there is no outer loop; with it, cross-vendor assurance happens without any owner having to share raw data they don't want to share.

The audit demonstrated on M100 is the offline version of the inner loop running against an archive instead of live telemetry. The same primitives, same state representation, same update rule. Pilots that run the inner loop only are the shortest path to value for a single-owner site; multi-vendor sites get the bigger operational lift from running both.
